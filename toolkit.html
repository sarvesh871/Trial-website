Exercise 1 Reference

1. Triple Bottom Line (TBL)

Coined by John Elkington, the Triple Bottom Line asserts that companies must measure performance against three interconnected dimensions: Planet (ecological stewardship), People (social responsibility), and Profit (economic viability).

True Venture Sustainability = Planet ∩ People ∩ Profit

Neglecting environmental externalities risks sudden regulatory bans or carbon liabilities; neglecting social conditions creates high turnover and safety hazards; neglecting unit profit results in insolvency.

Exercise 2 Reference

2. Business Model Decoupling

Linear hardware business models require proportional additions of labor and capital for every unit produced. Scalable business models utilize three core levers across Osterwalder's 9 blocks to decouple growth:

  • Automation: Replace manual labor bottleneck with robotic cell assembly and automated optical inspection.
  • Outsourcing: Convert fixed factory capital expenditures into variable per-unit contract manufacturing fees.
  • Platform Mechanisms: Enable decentralized installer networks and high-margin recurring IoT SaaS telemetry.
Exercise 3 Reference

3. Circular Supply Chains

Moving from traditional linear Take → Make → Waste topologies to closed-loop circular flows:

Linear: Extraction → Manufacture → Landfill
Circular: Recycled Sourcing → Modular Assembly → Take-Back → Hydrometallurgical Recovery

Anticipates volume breakpoints where single-supplier quotas fail or battery fire codes require certified 3PL hazardous-materials distribution.

Exercise 4 Reference

4. Unit Economics Framework

Unit economics assess whether an individual customer or product transaction creates economic value after factoring in acquisition and delivery costs:

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) = Total Sales & Mktg ÷ New Customers
Customer Lifetime Value (LTV) = Gross Margin × Lifespan + Recurring ARR
Healthy Venture Threshold: LTV ÷ CAC ≥ 3.0x

A high CAC without recurring revenue causes cash crunches as order volumes scale up.

Exercise 4 Reference

5. Break-Even Analysis (BEP)

The contribution margin method separates fixed overhead costs from per-unit variable costs to determine the exact volume where total revenues equal total expenses:

Unit Contribution Margin (CM) = Price (P) − Variable Cost (VC)
Contribution Margin Ratio (CMR) = CM ÷ Price
Break-Even Point (Units) = Total Fixed Costs ÷ Unit CM
Break-Even Point ($ Revenue) = Total Fixed Costs ÷ CMR
Exercise 5 Reference

6. Probability–Impact Risk Matrix

A structured prioritization tool that rates risks on a 1–5 scale of likelihood (Probability) and consequence (Impact):

Risk Score = Probability (1–5) × Impact (1–5)
Extreme (20–25) | High (12–16) | Medium (6–10) | Low (1–5)

Mitigation focuses on lowering Probability; contingency plans focus on reducing Impact; early-warning triggers provide quantified thresholds for executive intervention.

Exercise 6 Reference

7. ESG Reporting & Materiality

Translating sustainability promises into empirical key performance indicators:

  • Materiality: Focusing only on indicators that materially affect enterprise value creation or severe stakeholder harm.
  • GRI (Global Reporting Initiative): Broad external impact on society and ecology.
  • SASB (Sustainability Accounting Standards Board): Industry-specific financial materiality.
Exercise 7 Reference

8. Dunbar's Number & Culture Codification

Anthropologist Robin Dunbar demonstrated that human social networks have predictable cognitive coordination ceilings:

~15 People: Intimate founding squad; unwritten shared habits.
~50 People: Coordination limit for informal consensus; requires middle management.
~150 People: Dunbar limit; requires codified culture handbook, rituals & explicit governance.

Without deliberate culture codification, scaling past 50 employees results in fragmented silos, onboarding overload, and mission erosion.
